Now recruiting · 2027 positions

Advanced computational design, additive manufacturing and surrogate models for high-performance heat exchangers

We are inviting applications to join the MDAM Lab for a new research program on compact heat exchangers. The program addresses one research question: how far can the thermal performance of a heat exchanger be pushed without a matching penalty in pressure drop, while keeping it structurally sound and manufacturable?

The project will be conducted in collaboration with industry partners, connecting fundamental research with practical engineering challenges.

Location: University of Alberta, North Campus, Edmonton, Alberta

Geometric fidelity of signed distance field representations across the design-to-manufacture pipeline 4 monthsWinter 2027

Implicit modeling represents a part as a signed distance field rather than a boundary of faces. This makes Booleans, offsets, shells, blends and lattices robust, which is why it is increasingly used for additive manufacturing. But a real workflow moves between representations many times: a CAD part is sampled into a distance field (on a uniform grid or an adaptive one), the field is turned back into a triangle mesh for printing (by dual contouring or marching cubes), meshes are repaired and smoothed, and an optimized design is reconstructed as CAD. Each conversion can round sharp edges, close small holes, thin walls or move surfaces, and industrial users need to know by how much.

This project quantifies it. A benchmark set of parts (machined components with sharp edges and small holes, thin-walled castings, organic shapes and deliberately defective STL files) is passed through each conversion. Hausdorff and mean surface distances, volume and mass-property errors, sharp-edge and thin-wall preservation, watertightness and computing time are recorded against the sampling, accuracy and voxel settings. The outcome is a set of accuracy-versus-cost charts that link settings to achieved tolerance, and a catalogue of failure cases.

Geometric deviation and compressive behavior of additively manufactured polymer TPMS lattices 4 monthsWinter 2027

Lattice design tools predict a lattice's relative density, check whether it can be printed (minimum wall thickness, pore size, trapped resin or powder) and estimate its stiffness through homogenization. When a printed lattice is softer or stiffer than predicted, it is rarely clear whether the model is wrong or the print departs from the design.

This project separates the two for polymer TPMS lattices. Gyroid, diamond and primitive sheet lattices and one strut lattice are designed at three relative densities and two cell sizes, in uniform and graded form. They are printed by vat photopolymerization, following ASTM D1621 for the compression testing of cellular solids. The as-built relative density and wall thickness are measured (by mass and micro-CT or optical sections) and compared with the design. Quasi-static compression tests with digital image correlation give the modulus, plateau stress, densification strain and energy absorption, which are compared with the homogenized predictions before and after correcting for the as-built density. The study also tests whether printability checks correctly flag the specimens that fail to print.

Transferability of cantilever-calibrated inherent strains for predicting distortion in laser powder bed fusion 4 monthsWinter 2027

Parts made by laser powder bed fusion distort as they are built and again when they are cut from the build plate. The inherent strain method predicts this distortion within minutes: a strain, calibrated from a printed cantilever, is applied layer by layer in a linear elastic model. The method is fast enough for everyday use, but its calibrated strain is a fitted parameter, strictly valid only for the material and process parameters under which it was obtained. How well it transfers to other geometries is the question this project answers.

Calibration cantilevers are printed in one alloy to identify the inherent strain. The calibrated model then predicts the distortion of two validation parts (a bridge and a thin-walled bracket), which are printed and 3D-scanned on and off the build plate. Comparing the measured and predicted distortion shows how far a single calibration can be trusted for a new geometry.

Distortion, surface quality and removal effort of lattice supports in metal laser powder bed fusion 4 monthsWinter 2027

Supports hold overhanging surfaces, conduct heat away and stop parts from warping in laser powder bed fusion, but they cost powder and build time, and they often take longer to remove than the part took to print. Lattice supports (TPMS or strut lattices filling the support volume, with a thin contact interface to the part) promise a better balance, but their settings are mostly chosen by rule of thumb.

This project compares a small set of lattice supports, varying cell type and contact-interface thickness, against a conventional block support on a test part with flat and inclined down-facing surfaces. A single build is used to measure support printability, distortion of the supported surfaces, downskin roughness after removal, removal time and the mass of material fused. The outcome is a comparison of support settings and recommendations for default lattice supports.

ELITE Program for Black Youth

The Experiential Learning in Innovation, Technology, and Entrepreneurship (ELITE) Program, led by the University of Alberta with InnoTech Alberta and industry partners, places Black youth in paid internships of up to 8 or 16 weeks. Interns work on projects with university research labs, companies and government agencies in areas such as robotics, automation, advanced manufacturing and tissue engineering, with leadership, entrepreneurship and wellness training alongside. High school students, recent graduates and post-secondary students can apply.
